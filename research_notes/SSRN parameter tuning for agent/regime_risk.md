# SSRN evidence for regime filters and portfolio risk limits (India/crypto trading agent)

Scope: evidence to keep, tune or replace the agent's unverified regime gates (`src/regime.cpp`, `include/ta/regime.hpp`) and risk limits (`include/ta/risk.hpp`, `src/portfolio.cpp`, heat cap in `src/paper.cpp`). The current values are: `regime.trend_sma=200`; `regime.vol_lookback=250`; `regime.vol_red_pct=0.80`; `regime.realized_vol_n=30`; the crash gate fires at a 63-day return of -15% or worse plus a 21-day return of +10% or better (`crash_fall`, `crash_rebound`); `regime.event_days_before/after=1` (calendar days); sleeve `a1.drawdown_halve=0.075` and `a1.drawdown_off=0.15`; the active-book breaker halves at 10% and stops at 20%; the A1+A2 heat cap is 3% of the active book; `a1.risk_per_trade=0.004` and `a2.risk_per_trade=0.0025`.

## 1. Market-timing filters for momentum and swing strategies (200-day MA, momentum-crash state, volatility-scaled momentum, Indian evidence)

### Takeaway
A long moving-average trend filter reduces drawdowns but adds little or nothing to risk-adjusted return once look-ahead bias is removed. The agent should keep it as a cheap risk filter (`regime.trend_sma=200`) and should not tune its length. The momentum-crash literature defines the dangerous state differently from the current gate: it is a bear market measured over **24 months**, combined with **high trailing market variance (126 days)**, and the crash happens *during* the rebound. The current gate needs a +10% 21-day rebound before it turns red, which flags the state late. The best-supported change is continuous volatility scaling of the momentum sleeve. Momentum is the one factor family where volatility management survives real-time out-of-sample tests (9 of 9 momentum strategies).

### Cited Findings
**200-day / 10-month MA (Faber; Siegel; Zakamulin)**
- Faber timed the S&P 500 with a 10-month SMA over 1901–2012. The compounded return was 10.18% for timing against 9.32% for buy-and-hold, while the arithmetic averages were nearly identical (11.22% against 11.26%). The gain comes from lower volatility, not from higher average returns. — [Faber, A Quantitative Approach to Tactical Asset Allocation (SSRN 962461, 2013 update)](https://mebfaber.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/SSRN-id962461.pdf)
- In the same study, timing cut the 1929–32 drawdown from 83.66% to 42.24%. It also cut the 2000–02 drawdown to 16.52% against 44.73% for buy-and-hold, and it exited on 31 Dec 2007, avoiding the 50.95% drawdown of 2008–09. However, it "underperform[s] the index in roughly half of all years" and lags in roaring bull markets such as the 1990s. — [Faber](https://mebfaber.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/SSRN-id962461.pdf)
- Faber reports Siegel's 200-day SMA test on the DJIA (1886–2006). It bought when the index closed at least 1% above the SMA and sold when it closed at least 1% below. Siegel concluded that it improved risk-adjusted returns after all costs, including taxes, spreads and commissions, but trailed buy-and-hold on absolute return. Faber notes that Siegel's daily system trades about twice as often as Faber's monthly one. — [Faber](https://mebfaber.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/SSRN-id962461.pdf)
- Faber estimates that a monthly system averages "three to four round-trip trades per year" across the whole portfolio. He puts all-ETF fees at about 0.1%–0.3%. — [Faber](https://mebfaber.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/SSRN-id962461.pdf)
- Zakamulin (2018, *International Review of Finance* 18(2):317–327) argues that the "too good to be true" MA results reported by Glabadanidis (2015) come from look-ahead bias. Once the bias is removed, the MA strategy is "at best … only marginally better" than buy-and-hold and "in statistical terms … indistinguishable." — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/irvfin/v18y2018i2p317-327.html)
- Secondary summary of Zakamulin's longer studies, not read in full: they use US monthly data from 1860–2009 and a one-way cost of 0.25%. All rules tested were less risky than buy-and-hold (in cash about a third of the time), and their Sharpe ratios were generally higher. In out-of-sample tests of 300 weighting schemes, the simple momentum rule did best. — [search summary of CXOAdvisory / Zakamulin papers](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/market-timing-with-moving-averages-over-the-very-long-run)

**Momentum crashes (Daniel & Moskowitz)**
- The bear-market indicator I_B is an ex-ante variable equal to 1 "if the cumulative CRSP VW index return in the past 24 months is negative." The ex-ante volatility measure σ̂m is "the variance of the daily returns of the market over the 126 days prior." Momentum returns are "particularly poor during bear markets with high volatility." — [Daniel & Moskowitz, Momentum Crashes, NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- The crashes are rebounds. The two worst WML months were July and August 1932, at -60.98% and -74.36%, when the market was up about 33.6% and 36.5% in those months. March and April 2009 lost -42.28% and -45.52%. From March to May 2009 "the market was up by 26%, but the loser decile was up by 163%." — [Daniel & Moskowitz](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- In bear markets the momentum portfolio's up-market beta is more than double its down-market beta (-1.51 against -0.70). Hedging the market beta ex-ante does not avoid the crash: the "ex-ante hedged portfolio performs no better than the unhedged WML." — [Daniel & Moskowitz](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- The dynamic strategy, which weights by forecast mean over forecast variance, "approximately doubles the alpha and Sharpe Ratio of a static momentum strategy." It beats constant-volatility momentum in every quarter-century subsample (1927–49, 1950–74, 1975–99, 2000–13). Applied across markets and asset classes, it reaches a Sharpe ratio of 1.18, four times that of static US equity momentum. **Caveat:** the scaling constant λ and the normalising mean of σ̂m in bear states are set from the full sample, so this is not a clean out-of-sample test. — [Daniel & Moskowitz](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)

**Volatility-scaled momentum (Barroso & Santa-Clara; Cederburg et al.; Moreira & Muir)**
- Barroso & Santa-Clara (JFE 2015, sample 1927–2011) find that momentum risk is time-varying and predictable from its own realised variance. Scaling to a constant **12% target volatility**, using **126-day** realised volatility of daily WML returns, "virtually eliminates crashes and nearly doubles the Sharpe ratio," which rises from 0.53 to 0.97. Unscaled WML returned 14.5% a year. — [UCP record](https://ciencia.ucp.pt/en/publications/momentum-has-its-moments/); [r-bloggers replication note (126-day window)](https://www.r-bloggers.com/2015/09/hypothesis-driven-development-part-iv-testing-the-barrososanta-clara-rule/); Sharpe and target figures come from a [search-engine summary of Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/avoiding-momentum-crashes/) (secondary, full text not read). The summary quotes crash losses of -91.59% (1932) and -73.42% (2009). These are multi-month figures and do not conflict with Daniel & Moskowitz's monthly figures.
- Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang & Yan (JFE 138, 2020) test 103 equity strategies with real-time implementation. Volatility-managed versions have higher gross Sharpe ratios in only **53 of 103** cases, and only 8 of those are significant. Real-time mean-variance combinations that add a volatility-managed choice lower Sharpe and CER in **72 of 103** cases. The momentum group is the exception: **all 9 momentum strategies improve, 5 of them significantly**, which supports Barroso & Santa-Clara and Daniel & Moskowitz. — [Cederburg et al. (author PDF)](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf)
- Volatility scaling is leverage-hungry. Median exposure is about 1×, but "the 99th percentile of required leverage exceeds 400% in each case and reaches as high as 864% for the momentum strategy." — [Cederburg et al.](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf)
- Moreira & Muir (NBER w22208; JF 2017) report that volatility timing of the market gives a 4.9% alpha (4.86% annualised), an appraisal ratio of 0.33, a Sharpe ratio 25% higher than buy-and-hold, and a utility gain of about 65% of lifetime utility for a mean-variance investor. The alpha survives costs of 1 bp, 10 bp and 10+4 bp at a VIX of 40 (98th percentile). Variants with leverage capped at 1× or 1.5× trade less and are "much less sensitive" to costs. — [Moreira & Muir, NBER w22208](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22208/w22208.pdf)

**Indian evidence**
- Singh, Walia, Panda & Gupta (FIIB Business Review 11(3), 2022) study 450 BSE stocks. Relative momentum is "negatively skewed and prone to occasional severe losses." A risk-managed momentum approach "doubles the adjusted Sharpe ratio" and improves downside risk. The method, sample and cost treatment were not visible in the abstract. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/fbbsrw/v11y2022i3p347-358.html)
- Indian practitioner evidence: naive monthly-rebalanced momentum in India has suffered drawdowns above 50% "every 3–4 years." The same post says stop-loss, hedging and tactical overlays are paid for in higher transaction costs or forgone gains at turning points. — [StockViz, "Momentum without the Crash" (2021)](https://stockviz.substack.com/p/momentum-without-the-crash)
- An Indian study of beta-sorted portfolios found that technical (MA) rules beat buy-and-hold, but that "transaction costs erode much of the excess returns generated by shorter-lag strategies." This comes from a search summary; the window lengths were not seen. — [Business Perspectives, IMFI](https://businessperspectives.org/journals/investment-management-and-financial-innovations/issue-501/can-technical-analysis-create-returns-for-beta-based-portfolios-in-the-indian-market)

### Inferences
- **`regime.trend_sma` should stay at 200 and not be tuned.** The evidence supports a long MA as a drawdown and bear-market filter, with a modest or statistically insignificant Sharpe gain. That is the role it plays for a momentum sleeve, since Daniel & Moskowitz show momentum does worst in bear markets. Every alternative length tried (100, 150, 10-month) adds a trial to the deflated-Sharpe count, and the out-of-sample literature gives no reason to expect a better length. Two cheap changes reduce whipsaw without adding a free parameter beyond one trial: (a) evaluate the gate at the month-end close, as Faber does, rather than daily; or (b) add Siegel's ±1% hysteresis band. **Code change:** `trend_gate` currently flips on any close above or below the SMA; add a `regime.trend_band` (default 0.01) that requires price > SMA×(1+band) to turn green and < SMA×(1−band) to turn red. **Adds 1 trial.**
- **Redefine the crash gate (code change in `crash_gate`).** The current rule, a 63-day return ≤ -15% *and* a 21-day return ≥ +10%, is short-horizon. It also needs the rebound to have already happened, and in Daniel & Moskowitz the rebound months *are* the crash months. An ex-ante version closer to the evidence is: red when the **24-month (≈504-trading-day) Nifty return < 0 AND the 126-day realised index variance is above its trailing median or long-run mean**. The current fast trigger can be kept as an OR condition to catch V-shaped events such as March–April 2020, where a 24-month window can lag. Suggested keys: `regime.crash_bear_lookback=504`, `regime.crash_vol_n=126`, `regime.crash_vol_pct=0.5`. **Adds trials:** treat the definition switch as 1 trial and do not grid-search the lookbacks. Use the published 24m/126d values. The -15%/+10% values have no literature anchor; I found no source for them.
- **Replace binary halving with continuous volatility-scaled sizing for A1 momentum swing entries (code change).** Momentum is the only factor family where Cederburg et al. find a robust real-time benefit, and Barroso & Santa-Clara and Moreira & Muir give the mechanism. Implement a multiplier m = min(1.0, σ_target / σ̂_126) applied to `a1.risk_per_trade`. Here σ̂_126 is 126-day realised volatility of a momentum proxy, ideally the sleeve's own paper/live P&L or a Nifty200 Momentum 30 proxy, otherwise the Nifty. **Cap m at 1.0 (no leverage).** This follows the 864% leverage finding and Moreira & Muir's evidence that capped versions are cost-robust. σ_target is best set at roughly the long-run median of σ̂ so that m≈1 in normal times. New keys: `a1.vol_target`, `a1.vol_n=126`, `a1.vol_scale_cap=1.0`. **Adds trials:** 1 for the switch. Fix `vol_n` at 126 and set the target at the median rather than optimising it.
- Daniel & Moskowitz's doubling of the Sharpe ratio is in-sample-calibrated, so expect a smaller real-time gain. The robust part is the direction: cut momentum exposure in bear plus high-volatility states.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed, out-of-sample, net-of-cost test of a 200-day Nifty filter applied to Indian swing or momentum strategies. The Momo India 200-EMA test cited in the blueprint gives no numbers.
- Barroso & Santa-Clara's full text was not retrieved, so the 0.53→0.97 Sharpe and 12% target are from a secondary summary. I could not verify whether their results are net of costs.
- I did not obtain the method details of Singh et al. (2022): whether it is volatility-scaling or Daniel-Moskowitz-style, the sample years, and costs.
- I found no study of momentum crashes defined on Nifty bear/rebound states, and no source for the current -15%/+10% thresholds.

## 2. Volatility-percentile / VIX gates: do they improve out-of-sample risk-adjusted returns net of costs? India VIX evidence

### Takeaway
Volatility management in general does not systematically improve real-time Sharpe ratios: it wins in 53 of 103 equity strategies and lowers Sharpe/CER in 72 of 103 real-time combinations. It does reliably help momentum, and it thins left tails. No study I found tests a binary "top-20%-of-trailing-250-day India VIX → halve" gate out of sample, net of costs. Keep the gate as a risk control, not an alpha source. Prefer scaling on the absolute level of volatility over a trailing percentile, and do not tune `regime.vol_red_pct`.

### Cited Findings
- Real-time volatility management is not a free lunch. Most strategies do not gain out of sample; momentum is the exception (9/9 improve, 5 significantly). — [Cederburg et al. 2020](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf)
- Cederburg et al. say volatility management works when (i) volatility is persistent and (ii) the lagged-volatility/future-return relation is flat. Where high volatility forecasts high returns, scaling down hurts. — [Cederburg et al.](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf)
- For the US market factor, Moreira & Muir find volatility timing gives positive alpha after realistic costs, including costs that rise with VIX, and leverage-capped versions are less cost-sensitive. — [Moreira & Muir](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22208/w22208.pdf)
- In Daniel & Moskowitz, high trailing market variance (126 days) in a bear market forecasts low momentum returns, and the interaction term carries the predictability. — [Daniel & Moskowitz](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- India VIX: Thenmozhi & Chandra find an asymmetric negative relation between India VIX changes and Nifty returns. They suggest switching to large caps when VIX rises and to mid caps when it falls, and they argue India VIX captures volatility better than GARCH-class models. This comes from a search summary; out-of-sample and cost results were not seen. — [IIM Calcutta repository record](https://ir.iimcal.ac.in:8443/jspui/handle/123456789/3120?mode=full)
- Before the 2024 general-election result, India VIX rose to about 26.2, up about 96% in a month. It rose a further 24% on result day. — [Angel One](https://www.angelone.in/news/market-updates/election-result-day-and-market-volatility); [Moneylife (search summary)](https://moneylife.in/article/nifty-sensex-crash-on-election-surprise-tuesday-closing-report/74316.html)

### Inferences
- **`regime.vol_red_pct=0.80` should stay as is (no evidence-based tuning possible).** Each alternative percentile adds a trial. The percentile-of-own-year construction has a known flaw: after a long high-volatility regime (2008–09, 2020) the trailing 250-day distribution itself shifts up, so the gate can turn green while volatility is still high in absolute terms. Barroso & Santa-Clara and Moreira & Muir scale by the *level* of volatility relative to a fixed target, which has no such flaw. If the continuous `a1.vol_target` scaling from §1 is adopted, the binary vol gate becomes redundant for A1. It can then be kept only for the Sleeve C (options) stand-down, where a regime switch is the natural control.
- For A1 specifically, the evidence supports continuous scaling over a 0.5×/1× step. Cederburg et al. find momentum benefits robustly, and a step function is a crude approximation of 1/σ.
- `regime.vol_lookback=250` and `regime.realized_vol_n=30` should not be tuned. If realised volatility is used as the A1 scaler, align it to 126 days to match the literature, which counts as 1 trial when the scaler is introduced.
- For Sleeve B (crypto), Cederburg et al.'s condition (persistent volatility, flat risk-return) suggests volatility scaling of BTC/ETH trend is plausible, but I found no crypto-specific out-of-sample test in this pass.

### Gaps
- I found no out-of-sample, net-of-cost study of an India VIX percentile gate (any percentile) for swing, momentum or option-selling strategies. The NSE working-paper archive (nsearchives.nseindia.com) is blocked.
- I found no evidence on whether a percentile computed over 250 versus 500 days or the full history matters.
- I could not determine whether India VIX or realised volatility is the better input for the A1 gate in Indian data.

## 3. Drawdown-based risk control and stop-out rules at strategy and portfolio level

### Takeaway
Theory (Grossman & Zhou) prescribes exposure **proportional to the cushion above a drawdown floor**. The current two-step rules, halve at -7.5% and off at -15% for a sleeve, and halve at -10% and stop at -20% for the book, are a coarse discretisation of that linear rule with floors at -15% and -20%. They are defensible and should be kept. Empirically, stop-loss rules *lower* expected return under a random walk and add value only when returns are serially correlated (momentum/trend). Kaminski & Lo found 50–100 bp/month added during stop-out periods for US equities switching to bonds over 1950–2004. Out-of-sample evidence that drawdown-constrained portfolios beat simple benchmarks is weak. No study justifies the exact thresholds, so keep them and do not tune.

### Cited Findings
- Grossman & Zhou (Mathematical Finance 3(3), 1993) consider an investor who must not lose more than a fixed fraction of the running maximum wealth (W_t ≥ α·M_t). The optimal policy invests in risky assets in proportion to the surplus W_t − α·M_t, which equals the free Kelly fraction times the excess over the moving floor. The model assumes continuous rebalancing and no transaction costs. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v3y1993i3p241-276.html); description via [Palomar, Portfolio Optimization book §10.5](https://bookdown.org/palomar/portfoliooptimizationbook/10.5-drawdown-portfolios.html) (search summary)
- In an empirical illustration, drawdown-constrained portfolios built from 50 random S&P 500 stocks (200 draws, 2015–2020) "do not seem to outperform the simple GMVP benchmark" on Sharpe or max drawdown. The author notes more tests are needed. — [Palomar §10.5](https://bookdown.org/palomar/portfoliooptimizationbook/10.5-drawdown-portfolios.html) (search summary)
- Kaminski & Lo (J. Financial Markets 2014; working paper 2008): "under the Random Walk Hypothesis, simple 0/1 stop-loss rules always decrease a strategy's expected return, but in the presence of momentum, stop-loss rules can add value." With US monthly data from 1950 to 2004, "certain stop-loss rules add 50 to 100 basis points per month to the buy-and-hold portfolio during stop-out periods" when switching to long-term Treasuries. — [Kaminski & Lo, SIFR WP 63 abstract](https://swopec.hhs.se/sifrwp/abs/sifrwp0063.htm)
- Han & Zhou, "Taming Momentum Crashes: A Simple Stop-Loss Strategy" (2014): a 10% stop on each long/short leg, staying in cash until month-end, cuts the worst monthly loss of equal-weighted momentum from -49.79% to -11.34%. For value-weighted momentum it cuts the worst loss from -65.34% to -23.69%, over Jan 1926–Dec 2011. Average returns and Sharpe ratios "more than doubled," but the stops add trading costs. — [Crossing Wall Street summary](https://www.crossingwallstreet.com/?p=28384) (secondary; full text not read)
- Losses compound asymmetrically: recovering from a loss L needs L/(1−L), so 20% needs +25%. (Arithmetic; cited in the blueprint.)

### Inferences
- **Keep `a1.drawdown_halve=0.075` / `a1.drawdown_off=0.15` and active-book 0.10/0.20 (no tuning).** Under Grossman-Zhou with floor α=0.85 (sleeve) or 0.80 (book), the cushion-proportional exposure at the halve point is exactly 50% (7.5/15 or 10/20 of the cushion remaining). The current steps are therefore consistent with the theory's linear rule. The same applies to `b.drawdown_halve/off` for crypto, with the caution that crypto's higher volatility makes a 15% floor bind much more often.
- **Optional code change: replace the two-step rule with a continuous Grossman-Zhou multiplier** m_dd = clamp((floor_dd − dd)/floor_dd, 0, 1). An optional floor at 0.25 before "off" avoids micro-positions. This removes the cliff at -7.5% where exposure jumps from 100% to 50%. **Adds 1 trial.** The step version is cost-lighter, and the continuous version only matters if it is backtested.
- **The stop-out re-entry rule matters more than the threshold.** Kaminski & Lo's gains come from being out during *persistent* declines. For a trend or momentum sleeve, serial correlation supports a stop, which is the condition they identify. For mean-reverting sleeves such as A2 gap fades or Sleeve C short premium, the random-walk/mean-reversion case says hard stops subtract value. Off → paper-trading with a human reset is a reasonable re-entry rule. No literature sets the re-entry criterion. Consider re-enabling when the *paper* track regains its own high-water mark or after N months, logged as 1 trial.
- `a1.risk_per_trade` (0.004) and `a2.risk_per_trade` (0.0025), together with the 3% heat cap, have **no direct academic evidence** in this pass. Grossman-Zhou implies risk per trade should scale with the drawdown cushion, which the halve rule already approximates. Keep them, and do not add trials by tuning them.
- Combine multipliers multiplicatively and in a fixed order: risk_per_trade × m_vol (§1) × m_dd (this section) × gate factors. That prevents double-counting from becoming leverage. Each multiplier must be ≤1.

### Gaps
- I found no published out-of-sample study of multi-manager pod drawdown stops (e.g., -5%/-7.5%/-10% pod limits) with performance data. Industry thresholds are practice, not evidence.
- The Kaminski & Lo and Han & Zhou full texts were not retrieved, so thresholds tested, re-entry rules and cost assumptions are unverified beyond the abstracts and summaries.
- I found no CPPI or drawdown-control study on Indian equities or crypto.

## 4. Event-day effects in India (Budget, RBI, election results) and crypto (FOMC/CPI): justification for blackout windows

### Takeaway
Election results are the one Indian event with documented multi-day gap risk. Examples are -5.93% at the close (-8.52% intraday) on 4 June 2024, +17.74% (upper circuit) on 18 May 2009, and a roughly 20% fall within days of the 2004 results. Volatility builds for weeks before results. RBI policy surprises move equity volatility on announcement days. Budget effects are documented mainly as abnormal returns in ±15-day windows, from weak studies. A uniform ±1 **calendar**-day blackout is too short for elections and mis-handles weekend events: the 2009 result came on a Saturday and the market moved on Monday. Make blackout windows per event type and count them in trading days.

### Cited Findings
- **Elections, 4 June 2024:** Nifty fell 5.93% to 21,884.5, its worst day since March 2020. Intraday it was down as much as 8.52%, and India VIX rose 24%. — [Moneylife / Business Standard (search summary)](https://moneylife.in/article/nifty-sensex-crash-on-election-surprise-tuesday-closing-report/74316.html); [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/markets/news/stock-market-live-updates-june-4-sensex-nifty-lok-sabha-election-results-2024-psu-stocks-nse-124060400081_1.html)
- **Elections, history:** Nifty changes on result day or the next day were +1.06% in 1999, -1.64% in 2004, +17.74% in 2009 ("first trading day after results"), +1.12% in 2014 and -0.69% in 2019. India VIX touched about 26.2 before the 2024 result, up about 96% in a month. — [Angel One (fetched)](https://www.angelone.in/news/market-updates/election-result-day-and-market-volatility). *Conflict:* the same page gives the 2004 results date as both "May 01" (table) and "May 13" (text). The -1.64% figure does not capture the 2004 crash. Singh (2016) says that after the 2004 results "the Indian capital market plunged to 20 per cent and had hit the lower circuit," and that in 2009 it "rose to 20 per cent, hit the upper circuit." — [Singh, J. Asset Management 17(5), 2016, abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/assmgt/v17y2016i5d10.1057_jam.2016.23.html)
- Zerodha's study of five elections (1999–2019) found "almost always, the volatility tends to spike pre-election and cools off post-results," and result days were "volatile yet closed with muted performance," with 2009 the exception. — [Zerodha Z-Connect (fetched)](https://zerodha.com/z-connect/varsity/do-elections-impact-the-stock-market-here-are-our-five-observations)
- **RBI policy:** RBI Working Paper 03/2024 (Gupta, Pawar, Kumar, Borad & Seet) splits OIS moves on policy days into target and path surprises. Equity returns on announcement days respond only to the path factor, but **both** factors affect equity volatility. Intraday event windows confirm the path effect. — [RBI press release (search summary; page blocked)](https://website.rbi.org.in/web/rbi/-/press-releases/rbi-working-paper-no.-03/2024-equity-markets-and-monetary-policy-surprises)
- **Budget:** Patel et al. (arXiv 2502.15787, 2025) find "a significant negative trend in the cumulative average abnormal returns following the event date" for Budget 2024 on NIFTY50, with a ±15-day window over only four budget years (2020, 2022, 2023, 2024). This is weak evidence: four events and no volatility or gap statistics. — [arXiv 2502.15787 (fetched)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.15787). A practitioner tally puts the average Nifty return at -0.45% in the week before the Budget and +1.3% in the week after, since 2010. — [Bajaj Broking (search summary, low quality)](https://bajajbroking.in/blog/how-stock-markets-react-before-after-union-budget)

### Inferences
- **Code change: count `event_days_before/after` in trading days, not calendar days.** `EventCalendar::blackout` uses `days_from_iso` differences, so a Saturday result with `after=1` covers only Sunday and leaves the Monday reaction open, as in 2009. Use the index bar calendar instead, i.e. the n-th trading session before and after the event. **No trial cost:** this is a bug fix, not a parameter.
- **Make the windows per event type (code change: tag-specific windows in the calendar CSV or config):**
  - Elections (`regime.event_days_before.election`, `regime.event_days_after.election`): roughly 5 trading days before (pre-result volatility build-up) and 2–3 trading days after, to cover the 2004 day-2 crash. Evidence: 2004, 2009, 2024.
  - Union Budget: keep ±1 trading day for swing entries. The Budget's documented effects are smaller and diffuse.
  - RBI MPC: keep ±1, or 0 before and 1 after, since RBI WP 03/2024 shows volatility on the announcement day itself.
  - FOMC/CPI for crypto (Sleeve B, daily long/flat trend): the reaction lasts minutes, so a full-day blackout mostly costs trend entries. Use 0 days, and do not place orders in a ±1-hour window around the release time.
  - **Adds trials:** each per-event window is a choice. Fix the windows a priori from the evidence above and log them as 1 trial in total rather than grid-searching.
- The blackout should block *new* swing entries and new short premium. It should not force exits, since no evidence supports flattening existing trend positions before scheduled events. Gap risk for open positions is better handled by the existing 5% single-stock cap and gap-based sizing.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous Indian study of overnight gap size or realised volatility on Budget and RBI days compared with normal days, with standard errors. NSE research is blocked, and the RBI working-paper PDF could not be retrieved, so its magnitudes and sample are unknown.
- I found no study testing whether event blackouts improve out-of-sample net returns for swing strategies, in India or elsewhere. The case for blackouts rests on avoiding gap risk, not on performance evidence.
- The 2004 result-day figure is inconsistent across sources: Angel One says -1.64%, while Singh (2016) says the market fell about 20% within days.
